This is a really thorough breakdown, and your closing point -- that the two
approaches are complementary rather than competing -- is the part I agree with
most. Your banking example actually gives that argument even more support than
you claimed for it.

A funds transfer is a side-effecting sequence, so if Either short-circuits after
step three of four, you get a Left describing the failure, but the first two
database writes already happened. The short-circuit stops further computation
without undoing what's done. That seems like a good illustration of your point:
Java's try/finally paired with a transaction manager fits here because unwinding
the stack gives you a guaranteed place to hang the rollback, while functional
composition shines on the pure validation steps that come first -- account
exists, funds sufficient, limits respected. Scala's Resource and bracket
patterns exist for exactly this reason, which reinforces your observation that
real systems blend both models.

Your interop paragraph is well taken too. One detail I found interesting is that
scala.util.Try catches only NonFatal exceptions, so things like
OutOfMemoryError pass straight through. A sensible design choice, though it
means wrapping Java calls takes a bit more care than it first appears.

Building on your Melo et al. citation: if inconsistent exception handling is
partly a discipline problem, how much of it does a type system actually solve?
"case Left(e) => log(e)" compiles just as cleanly as
"catch (Exception e) { log(e); }". I'd be curious whether you see the functional
approach as genuinely preventing that, or mainly as making it easier to spot in
code review.
